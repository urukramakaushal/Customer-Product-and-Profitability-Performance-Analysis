# Customer, Product, and Profitability Performance Analysis in Supply Chain Operations

*A margin-intelligence study of 180,519 order lines at APL Logistics*

---

## Abstract

APL Logistics holds detailed order and sales data but lacks visibility into which customers, products and markets actually generate value. This study analyses 180,519 order lines across 40 fields, applies financial validation, and reports profitability by customer, product, category, market and discount level. On the validated book, revenue is $31.64M and profit $3.81M, a net margin of 12.03%. Three findings dominate. First, **profit is destroyed at scale before it is booked**: 18.7% of order lines lose money, erasing $3.71M — 49% of the $7.51M generated by profitable orders. Second, **discounting is a straight margin transfer with no measurable volume response**: the correlation between discount rate and units per order is +0.000, yet margin on gross sales falls from 13.0% at zero discount to 9.6% in the deepest band, and total discount spend of $3.57M is 94% of net profit. Third, and most consequential for how this analysis should be used, **margin does not persist at customer or product level** — a split-half reliability test returns a correlation of −0.29 across products, meaning realised margin in one half of a product's orders does not predict the other half. This finding invalidates the conventional recommendation to cull low-margin customers and products, and reframes where APL's margin opportunity actually lies.

**Keywords:** profitability analysis, margin erosion, discount diagnostics, customer value, supply chain analytics

---

## 1. Introduction

Supply chain analytics has historically optimised for speed and reliability — delivery windows, late-shipment rates, fulfilment cost. Those measures answer *how well do we deliver*. They do not answer *does delivering this profitably make sense*. A logistics business can hit every service target and still lose money on a substantial share of what it moves.

APL Logistics faces exactly this gap. The organisation possesses order-level financial detail but has not converted it into commercial intelligence. Pricing and customer strategy are consequently revenue-focused and profit-blind: decisions are made on sales volume, while the margin consequences remain invisible.

### 1.1 Research questions

1. What are total revenue, total profit and realised margin on a properly validated book of business?
2. How concentrated is profit across customers and products, and how much is destroyed by loss-making activity?
3. Do discounts pay for themselves through volume, or do they erode margin without compensating return?
4. Which product categories and markets are genuinely differentiated on profitability?
5. Which of these patterns are robust enough to act on?

Question 5 is not a formality. It turned out to be the question that governs the answers to all the others.

## 2. Data and Methodology

### 2.1 Dataset

180,519 order lines with 40 fields spanning order economics (gross sales, discount amount and rate, order item total, profit per order, profit ratio, quantity, unit price), customer identity and geography (customer ID, name, segment, street, city, state, country, zipcode, latitude, longitude), product hierarchy (product name and price, category ID and name, department ID and name), order geography (market, region, country, state, city), fulfilment (shipping mode, scheduled and actual shipping days, delivery status, late-delivery risk) and order lifecycle (order status, payment type).

**The dataset contains no order date field.** This is a material constraint: no time series, seasonality, cohort or trend analysis is possible. The "margin trend charts" specified in the project brief cannot be built as time trends. Section 3 substitutes trend analysis across discount level, price band and cumulative-contribution (Pareto) dimensions, which the data does support. Any genuine trend reporting requires the order date to be added to the extract.

### 2.2 Financial validation and cleaning

Rather than assume the financial fields are internally consistent, each accounting identity was tested directly.

| Validation test | Result |
|---|---|
| `Sales` = unit price × quantity | Holds for all 180,519 rows |
| `Order Item Total` = `Sales` − `Order Item Discount` | Holds for all 180,519 rows |
| `Benefit per order` = `Order Profit Per Order` | Identical — redundant column |
| `Sales per customer` = `Order Item Total` | Identical — redundant column |
| `Product Price` = `Order Item Product Price` | Identical — redundant column |
| `Order Profit Per Order` = `Profit Ratio` × `Order Item Total` | Holds within rounding for 94.5% of rows; max deviation $9.30 |
| Zero or negative sales, totals, quantities | None |
| Exact duplicate rows | None |
| Missing values | 8 in customer surname, 3 in zipcode — non-financial, retained |

Three of the forty columns are exact duplicates of other columns under different names. They add no information and were dropped from the analytical model.

**Commercially invalid orders were excluded.** 7,754 order lines carry the status `CANCELED` or `SUSPECTED_FRAUD`, representing $1.57M of nominal revenue and $160K of nominal profit. These transactions never produced realisable revenue. Including them overstates both the top and bottom line. All figures in this paper are computed on the remaining **172,765 order lines**.

The customer country field records the United States as "EE. UU." (Spanish abbreviation); this was normalised.

### 2.3 Measures

- **Revenue** is `Order Item Total` — gross sales net of discount. This is the amount actually invoiced.
- **Net margin** is profit ÷ revenue. **Margin on gross sales** is profit ÷ pre-discount sales, which is the measure that reveals discount erosion, since discount reduces the denominator of net margin and so partially conceals its own effect.
- **Customer Value Index (CVI)** is total profit ÷ unique customers.
- **Loss-order rate** is the share of order lines with negative profit.
- **Discount Impact Ratio** is expressed two ways: total discount as a proportion of profit, and the gradient of margin on gross sales across discount bands.

### 2.4 A reliability test on margin

Before drawing customer- and product-level conclusions, one question needs answering: is realised margin a stable property of a customer or product, or is it noise?

The test is a split-half reliability check. Each customer's orders were randomly divided into two halves and margin computed independently for each half. If margin is a real, persistent attribute, the two halves should correlate strongly. The same procedure was applied to products.

| Split-half correlation | Result |
|---|---|
| Margin across 8,419 customers with ≥10 orders | **+0.16** |
| Margin across 118 products | **−0.29** |
| Revenue across the same customers (control) | +0.25 |

Product-level margin correlates *negatively* with itself. A product's margin in one random half of its orders carries no information about its margin in the other half. Customer-level margin is weakly positive at best.

Supporting evidence points the same way. Mean profit ratio is essentially constant across every dimension available:

| Dimension | Range of mean profit ratio |
|---|---|
| 5 markets | 0.116 – 0.124 |
| 4 shipping modes | 0.117 – 0.127 |
| 3 customer segments | ~0.120 across all |
| 5 discount bands | 0.117 – 0.129 |
| 9 largest categories | 0.113 – 0.125 |
| On-time vs late delivery | 0.122 vs 0.120 |
| Correlation with unit price | −0.001 |
| Correlation with quantity | +0.000 |
| Correlation with discount rate | −0.002 |

The loss-order rate is likewise 18.3%–19.0% in every market, every segment and every discount band, and loss-making orders destroy 49%–50% of gross profit in every price band from under $50 to over $500.

**Interpretation.** Profit ratio in this dataset behaves as a value assigned independently of every observable business driver. In a real book, margin varies with cost structure, negotiated terms, product mix and freight — none of which shows here. This is the signature of a synthetically generated or heavily anonymised field.

This is reported prominently rather than in a footnote because it changes what the analysis can honestly support. Aggregate financial facts — total profit, discount erosion, the size of the loss-making tail — remain valid and important. Rankings of individual customers and products by margin do not, because they rank noise. A study that skipped this test would produce a confident list of "unprofitable customers to cull" that is statistically indistinguishable from a random selection.

## 3. Findings

### 3.1 Revenue and profit overview

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Gross sales (pre-discount) | $35.21M |
| Discounts granted | $3.57M (10.1% of gross sales) |
| **Revenue (invoiced)** | **$31.64M** |
| **Net profit** | **$3.81M** |
| Net margin | 12.03% |
| Margin on gross sales | 10.81% |
| Order lines | 172,765 |
| Unique customers | 20,261 |
| Customer Value Index | $187.87 per customer |
| Products | 118, across 50 categories |
| Countries served | 164 |

A 12% margin on a $31.6M book is thin but not alarming in isolation. The composition beneath it is the problem.

### 3.2 Profit is destroyed before it is booked

| Component | Value |
|---|---|
| Profit from profitable order lines | $7.51M |
| Loss from loss-making order lines | −$3.71M |
| **Net profit** | **$3.81M** |
| Share of order lines that lose money | 18.7% (32,295 lines) |

**Nearly one order line in five loses money, and together they erase 49% of the profit the business earns on everything else.** APL Logistics is operating a book where roughly half of all gross profit generated is consumed internally before it reaches the bottom line.

Set against discounting, the picture compounds: for every $1.00 of net profit APL keeps, it gives away $0.94 in discount and loses $0.97 on unprofitable orders.

The loss-making tail is not concentrated in cheap goods or any one geography. Loss-making orders destroy 49%–50% of gross profit uniformly across every price band, and the loss rate sits between 18.3% and 19.0% in every market and every segment. There is no subset of the business that can be isolated and fixed — which is precisely why the diagnostic in §2.4 matters.

### 3.3 Customer contribution

| Customer group | Share of revenue | Share of profit |
|---|---|---|
| Top 1% (202 customers) | 3.3% | 7.4% |
| Top 5% (1,013) | 13.5% | 28.7% |
| Top 10% (2,026) | 23.7% | 48.9% |
| Top 20% (4,052) | 40.5% | **79.0%** |
| Top 50% (10,130) | 72.1% | **121.0%** |

The Pareto pattern is textbook on the surface: a fifth of customers deliver four-fifths of profit, and the most profitable half of the base generates 121% of net profit — the loss-making remainder gives back the excess 21%.

4,001 customers (19.7% of the base) are net loss-making, collectively −$1.05M.

**Value tiers (profit quintiles):**

| Tier | Customers | Revenue | Profit | % of profit | Avg orders | Avg order value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 (highest) | 4,052 | $12.83M | $3.01M | 79.0% | 17.0 | $225 |
| Tier 2 | 4,052 | $7.87M | $1.30M | 34.2% | 10.7 | $235 |
| Tier 3 | 4,052 | $3.45M | $0.47M | 12.2% | 4.4 | $270 |
| Tier 4 | 4,052 | $1.33M | $0.08M | 2.2% | 2.2 | $129 |
| Tier 5 (lowest) | 4,053 | $6.18M | **−$1.05M** | −27.6% | 8.3 | $208 |

**The bottom tier is the analytically important row, and it does not behave as a bottom tier should.** Tier 5 customers place 8.3 orders on average at $208 per order — more orders than Tier 3 and Tier 4, at an average order value comparable to Tier 1. They are not marginal, low-engagement accounts. They generate $6.18M of revenue, 19.5% of the book. Their tier placement comes entirely from the margin realised on orders that are otherwise indistinguishable from profitable ones.

Combined with the split-half result in §2.4, the conclusion is that **the tier structure is substantially an artefact of margin noise, not a map of customer value.** The naive action — de-prioritise or exit the bottom quintile — would forfeit $6.18M of revenue from customers whose future margin the data gives no reason to expect will be negative.

Across segments, Consumer (51.9% of revenue), Corporate (30.4%) and Home Office (17.7%) all return margins within 0.1 points of each other. Segment is a volume distinction, not a profitability one.

### 3.4 Product and category profitability

Revenue is extraordinarily concentrated: **the top 5 products generate 60.6% of revenue and the top 10 generate 90.0%**, from a catalogue of 118 products across 50 categories.

**Top products by profit:**

| Product | Revenue | Profit | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Field & Stream Sportsman 16 Gun Fire Safe | $5.96M | $732K | 12.3% |
| Perfect Fitness Perfect Rip Deck | $3.81M | $474K | 12.4% |
| Diamondback Women's Serene Classic Comfort Bike | $3.55M | $410K | 11.6% |
| Nike Men's Free 5.0+ Running Shoe | $3.15M | $360K | 11.4% |
| Nike Men's Dri-FIT Victory Golf Polo | $2.71M | $334K | 12.4% |

**Products with negative total profit:**

| Product | Revenue | Profit | Orders | Unit price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SOLE E35 Elliptical | $26.4K | −$965 | 15 | $2,000 |
| SOLE E25 Elliptical | $9.4K | −$170 | 10 | $1,000 |

Only 2 of 118 products are net loss-making, both on order counts (15 and 10) far too small to distinguish a real margin problem from chance.

Category margins span 0% (Strength Training, 109 orders) to 19% (Golf Bags & Carts, 61 orders), but every category with meaningful volume sits between 11% and 13%. The apparent outliers are all low-count categories. The conventional deliverable here — "high-revenue, low-margin products requiring price action" — **returns an empty set**: no product with revenue above $200K has a margin below 11%.

The 90% revenue concentration in ten products is a genuine and strategically significant finding. The margin rankings beneath it are not actionable, for the reasons established in §2.4.

### 3.5 Discount impact diagnostics

This section contains the study's clearest actionable result.

| Discount band | Orders | Revenue | Discount | Profit | Margin on net revenue | **Margin on gross sales** | Profit per order | Avg units |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 9,640 | $1.97M | $0 | $255K | 13.0% | **13.0%** | $26.42 | 2.13 |
| 0–5% | 48,003 | $9.48M | $293K | $1.13M | 11.9% | **11.5%** | $23.45 | 2.13 |
| 5–10% | 38,386 | $7.21M | $617K | $882K | 12.2% | **11.3%** | $22.99 | 2.13 |
| 10–15% | 28,737 | $5.07M | $781K | $600K | 11.8% | **10.2%** | $20.88 | 2.13 |
| 15–20% | 38,424 | $6.44M | $1.39M | $756K | 11.7% | **9.6%** | $19.66 | 2.13 |
| 20–25% | 9,575 | $1.46M | $488K | $188K | 12.8% | **9.6%** | $19.64 | 2.13 |

Three observations.

**Margin on gross sales falls monotonically, from 13.0% to 9.6% — a 3.4 point erosion.** Margin on *net* revenue stays flat at roughly 12% throughout, which is exactly why net margin is the wrong metric for discount diagnostics: discount shrinks the denominator alongside the numerator and conceals its own effect. Any dashboard reporting only net margin would show discounting as harmless. It is not.

**Deeper discounts buy nothing.** Average units per order is 2.13 in every single band. The correlation between discount rate and order quantity is +0.000; between discount rate and profit ratio, −0.002. There is no volume response, no mix response, no unit-economics response. Profit per order falls steadily from $26.42 at zero discount to $19.64 in the deepest band — a 26% decline.

**The scale is material.** $3.57M of discount was granted against $3.81M of net profit. APL gives away 94 cents of margin for every dollar it keeps.

**Discount cap scenarios** (holding demand fixed):

| Cap | Discount recovered | Modelled profit | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15% | $401K | $4.21M | +10.5% |
| 10% | $1.09M | $4.89M | +28.5% |
| 5% | $2.10M | $5.91M | +55.2% |

These are upper bounds, not forecasts. The fixed-demand assumption is unusually defensible here given the measured zero volume elasticity, but historical data cannot show how customers would respond to a discount actually being withdrawn. A cap should be piloted on a subset of accounts before any book-wide rollout.

### 3.6 Market and regional analysis

| Market | Revenue | Profit | Margin | Share of revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Europe | $9.35M | $1.13M | 12.1% | 29.5% |
| LATAM | $8.83M | $1.07M | 12.1% | 27.9% |
| Pacific Asia | $7.14M | $0.83M | 11.6% | 22.6% |
| USCA | $4.35M | $0.54M | 12.4% | 13.7% |
| Africa | $1.98M | $0.24M | 12.1% | 6.3% |

Across 23 order regions, margin ranges only from 11% to 15%, with every high-volume region between 11% and 13%. Across 5 markets, the total spread is **0.8 percentage points**.

The brief asks for "markets with strong revenue but weak profit." On this data, that set is empty. Revenue differences across markets are large and real — Europe does 4.7× Africa's volume. Margin differences are not: a 0.8-point spread across five markets each containing over 11,000 order lines is within the range that order-level margin variation produces by chance.

**Geography is a volume lever at APL Logistics, not a margin lever.** Resource allocation decisions should be made on revenue scale and growth potential, not on the margin rankings, which carry no signal.

### 3.7 An operational note outside the financial scope

57.3% of shipments are recorded as late, against a mean scheduled duration of 2.93 days and mean actual duration of 3.50 days. Late delivery correlates with margin at effectively zero (mean profit ratio 0.120 late vs 0.122 on-time), so it has no visible financial consequence in this data. It is nonetheless a service failure on a majority of orders and warrants attention in its own right, through the operational analysis this study complements.

## 4. Discussion

**The margin problem at APL Logistics is structural, not selective.** The instinct behind the project brief — find the bad customers, the bad products, the bad markets, and fix them — presumes that unprofitability is concentrated somewhere identifiable. It is not. The loss rate is 18.7% everywhere: every market, every segment, every price band, every discount level. There is no bad neighbourhood to clean up.

**What is selective, and what is fixable, is discount policy.** Discount is the one lever where the data shows a clear, monotonic, large-magnitude relationship with margin, and where the counter-argument — that discounts buy volume — is directly falsifiable and directly falsified. A 10% cap models to a 28.5% profit improvement. Even at a fraction of that realisation, it exceeds anything available from customer or product culling.

**The reliability test is the study's most valuable output.** Standard profitability analysis on this dataset produces a bottom customer quintile losing $1.05M and recommends acting on it. That recommendation would forfeit $6.18M of revenue — 19.5% of the book — from customers who order as often and as large as profitable ones, on the basis of a margin figure that does not replicate. Reporting the ranking without testing its stability would have been the more impressive-looking deliverable and the more damaging one.

### 4.1 Limitations

- **No order date.** No trend, seasonality, cohort or lifetime-value analysis is possible. This is the single most valuable addition the extract could receive.
- **Profit ratio is not driver-linked.** Established in §2.4. Aggregate financials hold; entity-level margin rankings do not.
- **No cost decomposition.** Profit arrives as a single figure with no split between COGS, freight, handling and overhead. Which cost line drives the loss-making tail cannot be determined.
- **No order identifier.** Each row is an order line; multi-line orders cannot be reassembled, so order-level economics (including shipping cost amortised across a basket) are unavailable.
- **Discount elasticity is unobserved, not proven absent.** Zero measured correlation in historical data is consistent with genuinely inelastic demand, but also with discounts being granted on a basis unrelated to negotiation.
- **Customer identity is partial.** Customer ID with name and address, but no account hierarchy, so subsidiaries of one commercial relationship may appear as separate customers.

## 5. Recommendations

**1. Cap discretionary discounting and require approval above the cap.**
*Basis:* Margin on gross sales falls 13.0% → 9.6% across bands; zero correlation with order quantity; $3.57M granted against $3.81M profit.
Model a 10% cap first (+28.5% profit at fixed demand). Pilot on a defined subset of accounts and measure actual volume response before extending. This is the only recommendation in this paper supported by a large, monotonic, mechanism-clear relationship.

**2. Report margin on gross sales, not on net revenue.**
*Basis:* Net margin is flat at ~12% across all discount bands and conceals a 3.4-point erosion.
This is a one-line change to reporting definitions that makes the discount problem visible to every commercial decision-maker in the business. It costs nothing.

**3. Investigate the loss-making tail at cost level, not at customer level.**
*Basis:* 18.7% of lines lose money, destroying 49% of gross profit, uniformly across every observable dimension.
Because the tail is not concentrated in any customer, product or geography, it cannot be addressed by exit or repricing decisions. It requires cost decomposition — freight, handling, returns, payment terms — which this extract does not contain. Obtain that decomposition.

**4. Do not cull the bottom customer quintile.**
*Basis:* Tier 5 places 8.3 orders at $208 average value against Tier 1's 17.0 at $225; split-half margin correlation +0.16.
The tier's negative profit does not replicate and does not reflect ordering behaviour. Acting on it would forfeit $6.18M of revenue for no defensible expected margin gain.

**5. Treat the 90% revenue concentration in ten products as a risk register item.**
*Basis:* Top 10 of 118 products = 90.0% of revenue; top 5 = 60.6%.
This concentration is real and unambiguous. It is a supply, sourcing and dependency exposure independent of any margin question, and it should be managed as one.

**6. Add order date and cost decomposition to the data extract.**
*Basis:* §4.1.
Every question the brief asks about trends is unanswerable without dates, and the largest single profit leak — the loss-making tail — is undiagnosable without cost lines. These two additions would unlock more value than any further analysis of the current extract.

---

## 6. Conclusion

APL Logistics turns $35.21M of gross sales into $3.81M of profit. Between those two figures sit $3.57M of discount and $3.71M of losses on 18.7% of order lines — together consuming more value than the business retains.

The discount leak is measurable, mechanism-clear, and actionable: deeper discounts demonstrably buy no additional volume while eroding margin on gross sales by 3.4 percentage points, and a 10% cap models to a 28.5% profit improvement.

The loss-making tail is equally large but differently shaped. It is uniform across every dimension the data exposes, which means it cannot be addressed by deciding which customers, products or markets to keep. It is a cost-structure question, and answering it requires data APL has not yet supplied.

The study's final finding is a caution about its own genre. Applied uncritically, profitability segmentation on this dataset yields a confident list of customers and products to cut. Tested for reliability, that list dissolves — product-level margin correlates −0.29 with itself. The commercial intelligence APL needs is not a longer ranking. It is knowing which rankings mean something, and this analysis is explicit about which of its own do.
